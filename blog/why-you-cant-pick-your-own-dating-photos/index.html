Why You Can't Pick Your Own Dating Photos (the Science)

July 10, 2026 · 6 min read · by Muhannad Ahmed

You’re measurably worse at picking your own dating photos than a stranger is. In a 2017 UNSW study of over 600 people, photos selected by strangers made stronger first impressions than the ones people chose of themselves — notably on trustworthiness and competence. The reason isn’t vanity. It’s that you’ve never actually seen your face the way other people do.

The study: strangers beat you at your own photos

In 2017, researchers David White, Clare Sutherland and A. Mike Burton published “Choosing face: The curse of self in profile image selection” in Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications. The setup was simple and a little brutal. A group of 102 people each took 12 photos of themselves from their own Facebook and picked the ones they’d most (and least) want as a profile image for three contexts: a dating site, a professional network, and a social network. Then they did the same task with 12 photos of a complete stranger.

Hundreds of online raters — people who had never met anyone in the photos — then judged those images on attractiveness, trustworthiness and competence.

The result, in the authors’ words: “other-selected images conferred more favorable impressions than self-selected images.” When a stranger picked your photos, raters saw a significantly more trustworthy-looking and more competent-looking person than when you picked them yourself. In the full results, the stranger-selection advantage on trustworthiness and competence was substantial (effect sizes around ηp² = 0.10 for each), and stranger-selected photos also lined up better with how unfamiliar viewers actually rated the full photo set. A 2021 correction re-ran part of the analysis after a data-processing error was found — and the key effect got slightly stronger, not weaker. The authors’ blunt conclusion stands: “self-perception places important limits on facial first impressions formed by others.”

One honest wrinkle worth keeping: on raw attractiveness, strangers’ picks weren’t significantly better than your own. Where you lose is on everything else a match reads in your face — does this person seem warm, trustworthy, like they have their life together? Those are exactly the judgments that decide whether someone keeps reading your profile, and they’re the ones your self-picks undersell.

The dating context had its own tell, too. People selecting for a dating profile leaned hardest into attractiveness — the accentuation of attractiveness was roughly twice as large for dating picks as for professional ones. Which means the common instinct is to optimize for “hottest photo” while accidentally deprioritizing the trust signals that strangers pick up on naturally.

Why your own face disqualifies you as a judge

The UNSW result feels wrong until you look at what decades of face-perception research says about self-perception. Two findings do most of the explaining.

You’ve been staring at the wrong face your whole life

Every face you’ve ever seen of yourself in a mirror is horizontally flipped. In a classic 1977 experiment, Mita, Dermer and Knight printed each participant’s portrait twice — once as the camera saw it, once mirror-reversed — and asked which they preferred. People reliably preferred their mirror image. Their friends, shown the same two prints, preferred the true image — the one the rest of the world sees. That’s the mere-exposure effect: we like what we’ve seen most often, and you’ve logged thousands of hours with a reversed copy of your face that nobody else has ever met.

So when a photo “just doesn’t look like you,” that feeling is often accurate in the least useful way possible: it doesn’t look like the flipped face you know. It may look exactly like the face your matches will see.

You remember yourself as better-looking than your photos

The second distortion runs the other direction. In 2008, Epley and Whitchurch morphed participants’ faces in small steps toward a more attractive composite and toward a less attractive one, then asked people to find their real face. Participants tended to identify a version enhanced about 10% toward the attractive composite as their actual face — and they located attractively enhanced versions of themselves faster in a lineup than their true image. The bias showed up for their own face and a close friend’s face, but not for strangers.

Put those together and the UNSW finding stops being surprising. You’re comparing every photo against an internal reference that’s flipped, subtly idealized, and saturated with years of exposure. A stranger has no reference at all — so they judge the only thing that matters on a dating app: the actual first impression the actual photo makes.

What this means for your dating profile

The practical upshot isn’t “you’re not attractive.” It’s that your photo-picking instrument is miscalibrated in specific, documented ways. The photo you keep skipping because your smile “looks weird” may be the one where you read as warm and at ease. The carefully-angled one you love may match your mirror-face and nothing else.

It also reframes the standard advice to “just ask a friend.” Friends help, but Epley and Whitchurch found the idealization bias extends to close friends’ faces too — your best friend carries a flattering internal model of you, just a milder one. The UNSW raters who outperformed self-selection weren’t friends. They were strangers, judging cold. Cold judgment is the thing your profile will face ten thousand times, so it’s the thing worth simulating.

A few moves that follow directly from the evidence: get outside opinions from people who don’t know you well, or from a tool that has no memory of you at all. Judge candidate photos on what strangers actually rated — trust and warmth signals like a clear, unobstructed view of your face and a genuine expression — not on which one flatters your mirror-self. And don’t sort purely by “most attractive”; the dating-context data suggests that’s the axis people already over-optimize, at the expense of the impressions that were actually undersold.

FAQ

Do other people really pick better photos of me than I do?

Yes — that’s a measured result, not a vibe. In White, Sutherland and Burton’s 2017 study, photos of a person selected by strangers made significantly more favorable first impressions — especially on trustworthiness and competence — than the photos that person selected of themselves.

Why do I look different in photos than in the mirror?

Your mirror image is horizontally flipped, and it’s the version you’ve seen almost exclusively. Mita, Dermer and Knight (1977) showed people prefer their mirror-reversed photo while their friends prefer the true one — mere exposure trains each side to favor the face they’ve seen most.

Am I more or less attractive than I think?

On average, slightly less than your self-image — but not by much. Epley and Whitchurch (2008) found people identify a version of their face morphed about 10% toward a more attractive composite as their real face, so your internal picture is mildly enhanced.

Should I ask friends to pick my dating photos?

It beats picking alone, but friends carry a milder version of the same idealization bias, since Epley and Whitchurch found the enhancement effect extends to close friends’ faces. The strongest signal comes from strangers or a neutral tool — the same cold, no-context judgment your profile actually gets.
