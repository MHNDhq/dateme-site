Choose Hinge photos like this: lead with a clear, solo shot of your face wearing a genuine expression. Fill the remaining slots with variety — one full-body, one activity, one social photo where you're easy to spot. Cut near-duplicates, sunglasses-in-every-shot, heavy filters, and group-only photos. Six slots, six different reasons to like you.

That's the whole system. The rest of this guide is why each rule exists, so you can apply it to your actual camera roll instead of guessing.

## Your lead photo does most of the work

People decide fast. Research on face perception has found that we form impressions of a stranger's character from [even a single glimpse of a photograph](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9796653/) — spontaneously, sometimes before we're aware we're judging at all. By the time someone consciously reads your prompts, their gut has already voted on your first photo.

So the lead photo has one job: show clearly, immediately, what you look like — and make that first split-second impression a warm one.

That means:

- **Solo.** Nobody should have to solve a puzzle to figure out which person you are. Hinge's own [profile guidance](https://hinge.co/newsroom/profile-refresh-guide) is blunt about this: no photos that hide your face, and go easy on group shots.
- **Face visible and unobstructed.** No sunglasses, no heavy shadow, no back-of-the-head artsy shot. Save those for slot five if you love them.
- **A genuine expression.** The same face-perception research found that smiling is one of the strongest cues people read as trustworthy. A real smile — the kind that reaches your eyes — beats a practiced smolder for most people, most of the time. If smiling with teeth feels fake on you, a relaxed, open expression works; forced is the only wrong answer.
- **Recent.** Hinge explicitly tells daters not to use outdated photos. A lead photo from four years and one haircut ago sets up a first date that starts with mild disappointment, which is the worst possible opening.

One more thing the research is honest about: these snap judgments have [limited accuracy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9796653/). People are not actually reading your soul from a JPEG. But the judgments happen anyway, and they're shaped by things you control — expression, lighting, camera angle. You might as well control them.

## Fill six slots with variety, not six angles of the same night

Hinge gives you six photo slots, and the mistake almost everyone makes is treating them as six chances to show the same thing. Five near-identical headshots don't quintuple your appeal; they tell people you have exactly one photo you like of yourself.

Think of the slots as answering different questions someone is silently asking:

1. **What do you look like?** The lead photo, as above.
2. **What do you look like, full length?** One clear full-body shot. Not a mirror pose — just you, standing somewhere, at a normal distance. Skipping this reads as hiding something, even when you're not.
3. **What do you actually do?** An activity photo — climbing, cooking, on stage, mid-hike, at your pottery wheel. Concrete beats generic: "person at a specific thing" gives someone an opening line.
4. **Do other humans enjoy your company?** One social photo, and only one — Hinge's guidance says to pass on more than one group photo. You should be instantly identifiable in it. If a stranger needs a caption to find you, it's not doing its job.
5. **A second clear face photo.** Different setting, different light, same recognizable you. Tinder's research points the same direction: in its [Photo Selector announcement](https://au.tinderpressroom.com/news?item=122591), the company reported that men who include more than one clear face photo were 71% more likely to match with women. One face photo can be a fluke; two is a pattern.
6. **A wildcard.** The travel shot, the dog, the ridiculous Halloween costume. Personality lives here.

You don't need all six to be perfect. You need them to be *different*. Two photos that could be crops of each other are one photo wearing a disguise — cut the weaker twin.

## What to cut, ruthlessly

Some photos cost you more than an empty slot would:

- **Sunglasses in every shot.** One pair of sunglasses in one photo is a sunny day. Sunglasses in most photos means nobody has seen your eyes, and people notice.
- **Group-only lineups.** If every photo has four people in it, you're asking strangers to do detective work. They won't.
- **Heavy filters and obvious retouching.** The face-perception literature is clear that people read trustworthiness holistically from faces — skin, expression, lighting together. A filter that smooths you into a render undermines the exact signal you're trying to send, and it sets up an in-person reveal you don't want.
- **Bathroom and gym mirror selfies.** Hinge's official guide singles out gym selfies specifically. The problem isn't your body — it's that mirror selfies are the photographic equivalent of talking about yourself in the third person.
- **The ex-shaped crop.** A disembodied hand on your shoulder raises questions no prompt can answer.

When in doubt, ask: would I be comfortable if this photo were the *only* thing someone saw? If the answer is no, it shouldn't be in the six.

## Order matters more than people think

Photo order isn't cosmetic. Tinder built an entire feature, Smart Photos, around automatically reordering profile photos — and reported that in testing, members saw [up to a 12% increase in matches](https://www.tinderpressroom.com/introducing-smart-photos-for-the-most-swipeworthy-you) from reordering alone. Same photos, same person, different sequence, measurably different results. Hinge runs the same play with its [Top Photo feature](https://hinge.co/newsroom/profile-refresh-guide), which continuously tests your pictures and puts the most popular one first.

The logic to steal from both platforms: your strongest, clearest solo photo goes first, and the sequence should build a picture rather than repeat one. A reasonable default order is face → full body → activity → social → second face → wildcard. Front-load clarity, back-load personality.

And if the platform offers to test your order for you, let it. The photo you're attached to and the photo that performs are frequently not the same photo — Tinder's own research found [52% of singles say choosing a profile image is hard](https://au.tinderpressroom.com/news?item=122591), and 18-to-24-year-olds spend an average of 33 minutes trying. You are, officially, too close to your own face to judge it.

## The honest test

Before you publish, show your six photos to one blunt friend and ask two questions: "Do these look like me on a good day?" and "Would you recognize me from the first photo alone?" If either answer wobbles, swap photos until it doesn't.

Notice what's *not* in this guide: nothing about being more attractive than you are. The goal is a profile where the person who shows up to the date is the person in the photos — just clearly lit, genuinely smiling, and easy to find in the group shot.

## FAQ

**What should my first Hinge photo be?**
A recent, solo, unobstructed photo of your face with a genuine expression — no sunglasses, no group, no heavy filter. People form impressions from a single glimpse, so clarity and warmth in slot one matter more than anything else on your profile.

**How many group photos should I have on Hinge?**
One, at most. Hinge's own guidance says to pass on more than one group photo, and you should be instantly identifiable in the one you keep.

**Does the order of dating profile photos actually matter?**
Yes. Tinder reported up to a 12% increase in matches in testing when its Smart Photos feature simply reordered members' existing photos. Put your clearest solo face photo first and vary what each photo shows after that.

**Should I smile in my dating profile photos?**
Generally, yes — face-perception research consistently finds smiling is one of the cues people read as trustworthy in photographs. If a toothy grin feels unnatural, a relaxed, genuine expression beats a forced smile.
